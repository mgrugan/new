Lecture 11

Elasticity of Supply

How much quantity supplied responds when price changes, and why it matters for every market analysis.

The idea in one sentence

Price elasticity of supply = % change in quantity supplied ÷ % change in price. It tells you how flexible producers are.

💡 Intuition: Picture a bakery and a vineyard. If bread prices jump, the bakery can bake more tomorrow (elastic). If wine prices jump, the vineyard has to plant vines and wait years (inelastic). Elasticity of supply really comes down to how easily can producers adjust?
ValueNameWhat it looks like
> 1ElasticFlat-ish supply curve: small price change → big quantity change
< 1InelasticSteep supply curve: big price change → small quantity change
= 0Perfectly inelasticVertical line: quantity is fixed (e.g., original Picasso paintings, seats in a stadium, beachfront land)

Determinants (what makes supply flexible?)

  • Ability to store inventory: stored goods can be released when price rises.
  • Availability of inputs: easy-to-get inputs mean easy expansion.
  • Spare capacity: idle factories or machines make expansion easier.
  • Entry and exit: if new sellers can enter quickly, supply is elastic.
  • Time: the longer the time frame, the more elastic supply becomes.
🥚 The egg example: When egg prices spiked, retail orders went up 6× but total egg sales only rose 44%. Retailers could pull eggs away from wholesale buyers (bakeries, restaurants), which are a substitute in production, so retail supply was very elastic. Total supply depends on hens, and you can't grow hens overnight, so total supply was much more inelastic.

🎛️ Try it: why relative elasticity matters

When demand shifts, you move along the supply curve, so supply elasticity decides the quantity change. When supply shifts, you move along the demand curve.

Lecture link: with inelastic demand and elastic supply (the heroin market), shifting demand changes quantity a lot. Cutting supply mostly just raises the price.

Lecture 12

Government Policy: Quantity Regulations

Governments step into markets with quantity rules, price ceilings, price floors, taxes and subsidies. This lecture covers quantity rules.

PolicyWhat it doesBinding when…
Quota (maximum quantity)Caps how much can be soldThe cap is below the equilibrium quantity
Mandate (minimum quantity)Requires at least some amount (e.g., required car insurance, ethanol in gasoline)The minimum is above the equilibrium quantity
💡 "Binding" just means "it actually changes something." A speed limit of 200 mph doesn't bind anyone. A quota of 25,000 taxis when only 20,000 would operate anyway also binds no one.

🚕 NYC Taxi Medallions (Haas Act, 1937)

  • Medallions capped at 11,787 (today 13,857). Originally cost about $10.
  • Goals: support driver incomes after the Depression and cut congestion.
  • In the lecture example, equilibrium is $15/ride with 20,000 taxis. A 25,000 quota is not binding. A 12,000 quota is binding.
  • A binding quota → fewer rides → higher price. The gap between what riders will pay and drivers' cost is a quota rent, and that rent is what makes the medallion valuable (they sold for about $1M at the peak).
  • Population grows → demand rises, quantity can't → price and medallion value rise even more.
  • Uber arrives → a substitute → taxi demand falls → price and medallion value crash.

Lecture 13

Price Ceilings & Price Floors

Legal maximum and minimum prices: rent control, milk prices and the minimum wage.

⬇️ Price Ceiling (maximum price)

  • Binding only if set below equilibrium.
  • Result: shortage (Qd > Qs).
  • Example: rent control. The goal is affordable housing.
  • Unintended effects: fewer apartments, lower quality and upkeep, waiting lists, people staying put, black markets, and benefits that go to whoever already holds a unit.
  • NYC data: the wealthiest renters saved 39% and the poorest saved 15% (2019 WSJ).

⬆️ Price Floor (minimum price)

  • Binding only if set above equilibrium.
  • Result: surplus (Qs > Qd).
  • Examples: milk (PA $4.28, ME $4.06) and the minimum wage ($7.25 federal).
  • In the labor market a surplus of labor = unemployment.
  • But Card & Krueger (1994) found that NJ's minimum wage increase did not reduce fast-food jobs compared with PA (Nobel 2021).
💡 Memory trick: A ceiling is above your head, but it only matters if it's low enough to hit you, so it binds below equilibrium. A floor is under your feet, but it only matters if it's high enough to push you up, so it binds above equilibrium. Ceilings cause shortages and floors cause surpluses.

🎛️ Try it: drag the legal price

Equilibrium is $30 with 40 units. Try a ceiling above $30 or a floor below $30, and notice that nothing changes.

🤔 Why might the minimum wage not cut jobs?

  • Monopsony: when a few employers dominate a local labor market, they pay below the competitive wage. A minimum wage can then raise employment.
  • Lower turnover: better-paid workers quit less, which saves hiring and training costs.
  • Higher productivity and morale (efficiency wages).
  • Inelastic labor demand: firms may pass costs on to prices instead of cutting staff.

Real vs nominal: $7.25 hasn't changed since 2009 in nominal dollars, but inflation means its real value keeps falling. If it had tracked productivity after 1968, it would be about $21.50. Today it sits below the 2-person poverty-level wage ($9.16).
